--- title: A much-needed rest day summary: The schedule and routine has been intense! tag: post date: 2026-08-12 ---

Today is what's called a 'Consolidation Day'. We were granted an extra sleep-in, and so started getting ready at 6:30am, rather than the usual 5:15am. [This is in stark contrast to my lifestyle of the last two years, which would see me only rising from bed after 9am.]

Today is the day to make sure our logbooks and workbooks are up to date with all the answers and essays we need to produce for the module on 'How to Guide'. A rather dry subject, but critical none-the-less.

I also used the 'free' time today to wash my socks and underwear.

The Grind

The daily routine this week has involved:

  • Morning coffee/tea
  • Early morning walks or drives (depending on which group you're in - I'm a Honey Badger). Each activity is 3-4 hours long, and involves us studying the various rocks, animals, plants, tracks, and other bushveld signs as we course through the bush.
  • Breakfast
  • 1-2 hours of lectures on the given topic of the week
  • 2-3 hours of self-study time, or filling in our logbooks
  • Late lunch
  • An afternoon walk/drive, mirroring the morning activity
  • Dinner

On one particular night this week, after driving quite far south, our Instructor Godwin switched off the vehicle, and turned off the lights. For the next few minutes, we all sat in a stunned silenece looking up at the stars, distracted by the satelites and meteors.
This too was a teaching moment, as Godwin pointed out the constellation of Scorpio, even going as far to point out Antares, the Super Red Giant that could go supernova at any time soon, cosmologically speaking.

Mammal sightings have been few on our guided walks, so we've been focusing a lot on the trees, flowers and droppings as we approach them. I suspect we are either too noisy, or too smelly, or both. Maybe that's why we're not seeing many animals.
But one particular interaction was quite magical…

We were studying the Knobthorn tree, talking about its varied medicinal and cultural uses, its characteristics, etc. when a bright blue flash of wings appeared atop the tree, and started making an alarm call. The ever-gorgeous Lilac-Breasted Roller was telling us off for being too close to its nesting site, and warning its mate about our presence, as just adjacent to the Knobthorn were several cavities in a dead tree, one possibly containing its nest.

We retreated shortly after, but it's always a wonderful feeling when wildlife comes up to us, rather than typically scurrying away.

Another night-time encounter

Harold had taken us far south in one of the Land Cruisers, following the Pitsane river bed towards the Pitsane ridge, where the cold winds and dying light meant we had to eventually pack up and head back to camp.

As we're boarding, Michaèl (The Boy Who Cried Wolf) said he heard something, which we all dismissed immediately; he persisted.

So Harold and Michaèl went to the edge of the ridge and listend again, and sure enough they both heard something and bolted back to the truck.

We sped down the hill and started scanning the terrain with our spotlights for the pair of yellow eye reflections we expected.

Just as we were about to give up, and leave the river bed for the adjacent roads, Michaèl spotted a faint set of eyes, about 100 meters up the riverbed.

We turned the vehicle up the riverbed and approached, and sure enough, there was a adult female Leopard, making rasping calls, looking for her cubs.

She paused to drink from the river, and later gracefully jumped the gap to cross the river.

We followed her for a while, but she lay down on the bank, so we decided to head back home before we overstayed our schedule and our luck.

Her calls evoke feeling of sadness, as it's been two days of her doing the same thing. I hope her cubs are well.

Our progress

Collectively, we've seen many species of plants and animals, which we've all recorded on a whiteboard in the lecture tent.

Some of the birds have been 'Lifers' for me, so it's been extra rewarding seeing them.

We haven't seen many reptiles, simply because its too cold for them at this time of the year. The frogs and toads have been plentiful, but it's been a bit complicated to see them so far.

Last night

After yet another wonderful dinner (Beef Stew), some of the group decided to set up the projector and screened a movie, but the rest of us were feeling the impact of the relentless pace, and excused ourselves.

As I was cleaning up in my tent I was distracted by a noise outside.
What follows was a mix of horror and fascination, as the soft crackling of leaves outside the tent gave away the animal's movements. Its pair of yellow-orange eyes shining like hell-fueled beacons, reflecting in my headlamp. Its posture slouched, and coat camoflaguing it well amongst the leafy Mopane trees.

Some of my camp-mates arrived, and the Spotted Hyena skulked away deeper into the bush.

Weeks prior their clan had raided the camp cupboard and drinks fridge in the lecture tent, and have been back almost every night since. [Cargo Culting Hyenas!]
