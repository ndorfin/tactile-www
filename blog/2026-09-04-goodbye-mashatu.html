--- title: Goodbye Mashatu, Hello Timbavati summary: It's been a wonderful month in Botswana tag: post date: 2026-09-04 ---

Goodbye Mashatu

I'm going to miss Botswana. It was almost a month in Mashatu Nature Reserve, and it wasn't until I had to research the history there as part of an assignment, that I really came to appreciate the depth and majesty of the Tuli Block and its inhabitants.

The Elephants were really shy, but they were around, and we got our fair share of amazing predator sightings such as Lions and Leopards.

The Cheetahs, Honeybadgers, Bat-eared Foxes, and many noctural species weren't to be seen though. We did see a Small-spotted Genet and evidence of Aardvarks and Porcupines, but nothing otherwise.

What was special though was because the grass was so dry (and well-groomed by the residents), we could walk more freely than normal, and see further into the bush. Mashatu is prominent in Mopani and Purplepod Clusterleaf trees, but they all suffer due to the low rainfall and alkaline soils. Giraffes are so easy to spot, becuase the Elephants regularly trim the Mopani down to shoulder height, as such, there are very few Mopani that look vibrant and lush.

Collectively, we saw more than 105 species of birds, including some new lifers for me such as the Black Stork, and Great Egret.

We (somewhat thankfully) didn't see many reptiles beyond Lizards/Geckos, Monitors. That's mostly because many of them are still hibernating, including all the venomous snakes.

I'm going to miss our three Motswana instructors and their unique style of teaching. It was calm and forgiving, but also deeply human.

Hello Timbavati

On the first of September, we took the Blessing Bus down to Timbavati, a trip that took just over 8 hours, including driving the long stretch of Mashatu down to the border.

It feels a bit like an upgrade of sorts here now.
The vehicles are newer, the common areas are a big upgrade (including sofas and a TV for watching doccos), as well as a pool and table tennis.

The bush is overgrown with thick grasses, but last night they started a controlled burn of the concession.

It was like a scene out of a war movie, fires raging all around us, and the smoke burning our eyes. Thankfully the wind continued as predicted, and the camp remained safe.
Right now, the amount of ash everywhere is crazy, and the smell acrid.

With the burning comes a chance for nature to start over. Many of the trees and grasses have already started sprouting, and it also meant an opportunity to photograph a new lifer for me, the Senegal Lapwings, as they scurried along the burnt veld looking for insects, something they specifically like doing.

Many bulbs and seeded grasses will sprout unhindered from the now removed overgrowth, as soon as the expected rains come.

We started our first guided walk today, and the unburned sections of the reserve are sometimes too thick to pass someone else on the game trails.

Most of the trees are recognisbale in some way from our time and learnings in Mashatu, but their shapes and sizes are different here.

The thick grass also means there are abundant Spurfowl, Finches, Waxbills (and other small seed-eaters) and grass nesters such as Cisticolas.

Even the instructors are different here. There's a pronounced strictness in Guided Walk discipline, and finally we're being instructured to keep noise to a minimum and be more aware of our footsteps and our surroundings.
As a result, we finally saw some larger mammals within a hundred meters today.

The birdsong is incredible here, and the carpet to the canopy is filled with Shrikes, Sunbirds, small insect eaters such as Pytillia and Eremomelas and more.

It's still early days here, and I'll check in a bit later. I hope you're all well.
