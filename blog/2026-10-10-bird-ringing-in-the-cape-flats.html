--- title: Bird Ringing in the Cape Flats summary: A morning out catching and ringing wild birds in the Cape Flats Nature Reserve tag: post date: 2026-10-10 ---

Laurenda, my friend from Cape Flats Nature Reserve let me know about a bird-ringing session they were conducting as part of their annual Biodiversity Survey, so I popped along to check out the whole thing.

It was beautifully foggy and cool in the morning.

We arrived to see Dieter and Muneeba arranging the mist nets along the walking paths. Mist nets are extremely thin, and very difficult to see if you're buzzing along the bushtops as a bird does.

It's a delicate operation to set them up, and some of the nets were tangled a bit, but all we could really do was hand the two more experienced ringers the appropriate tools as they erected each tent pole and net.

After most of the nets were set up, some of the more active birds such as weavers started getting caught. And the Southern Fiscals were ever present, seeing if it could prey on the compromised birds in the nets.

It didn't take long before one of the Red-faced Mousebirds got caught in the nets too.

Seeing them up close was fascinating; their hairy feathers have a green tinge to them, depending on their position relative to the sun.

Zafar set about ringing the mousebird, being extra careful with the way he held it, placing its neck between his two fore fingers, and then cupping the bird with the rest of his hand. Bird lungs are very sensitive, and they don't have much protection in the way of ribs.

We would take some measurements of the wings and feathers, and then put them in a cup, head first, and weigh them. The sex, and any abnormalities would be noted (as well as inspecting the Weaver's primary feathers for moulting as part of Dieter's research).

After a quick photo, they'd be released, and hopefully not get caught in the nets again.

Other traps

Dieter set up a 'clap trap' for the nearby Shrike, baited with a mealworm, and sure enough it caught one of the Fiscals.
This time, with rining it, Muneeba was really careful not to get bitten, as they have fierce beaks, but it still managed to nab her on one of her fingers.

Once we had a sizeable portions of birds rung, we packed up the nets and station, and headed down the road to see if we could find any birds of prey.

We spotted a Black-shouldered Kite on the left-hand side of the road, and Dieter sprang into action, carrying a dome-shaped talon-noose trap.

The trap has a number of fishing-line nooses tied to the top of it, so that when a rapter tries to grab the mouse inside it, its talons get stuck in the nooses. The mouse's name is Oreo by the way, and has been responsible for many successful raptors being caught.

Dieter placed the trap on the road where it could be seen by a raptor and we all moved off to give the birds some space. Unfortunately we didn't get to catch the Kite, but what an amazing way to end off the day.
