--- title: Advanced Rifle Handling summary: Sweating, aiming, drilling, and shooting tag: post date: 2026-11-08 ---

It's been a hot, sweaty, and intense week in Makuleke concession. On the bright side, it's been a birding paradise, and I managed to photograph plenty of new lifers.

PFTC and the basics

Lelanie from Limpopo Firearms Academy was our instructor for the first two days. On day one, we completed our Firearms Act law books and exams, and then she ran us through safety, theory, and our first practical exercises associated with the PFTC.

Early the next morning, we left camp at 4AM, and headed out to the range, a solid hour of off-road driving in two loaded game-viewing vehicles and one trailer.

I wasn't prepared for the unique spectacle that is Makuleke. Large Baobabs sprouting new leaves towering over the larger (than what we're accustomed to) Mopani trees, the early dawn colours, and birds, birds everywhere.

The range was a simplistic setup; a large sandy bank propped up by old car tyres, and enough space to shoot up to 20 meters if needs be.

We set up the gazebos, tables, targets at 7m, and parked the two cruisers so that we can tie up a tarp between them in preparation for the coming blaze of an aggresive tropical sun.

The rifles came out… a .22 Long, .308, and a 7.62x39mm bolt-action rifles, all with iron sights.

The .22 Long felt very familiar, as that's what we shot in school. It's light, and proved to be an excellent introduction to standing position shooting. It had a 10-round magazine.

After suiting up, and going through the safety drills, we shot our first salvo of 10 into the first target. We had to have all 10 rounds in the target.

We then had another 2 salvoes of 10 to do with the .308 and 7.62mm from 12m. The kick from those rifles was noticeable now, as was the sound, even through the ear protection.

And just like that, it was 10AM and boiling hot. So we packed up and headed back to camp.

After breakfast that day, we had some time to ourselves, and that afternoon we drove out east to sit by the Limpopo river, have some refreshments, and observe all the amazing forest, bushveld, and water birds.

Training for the FGASA assessments

The next few days were very similar, except we spent afternoons doing 'dry-drills'.

One of the assessments is being able to load, aim, and unload the 5-round magazine .375 bolt-action rifle blindfolded within a certain amount of time. It's principle is to train muscle memory and good habits.

The most daunting assessment is one in which a Lion (poster) is towed on a sled at roughly 40km/h towards you. You have to fire a brain shot while it is within 10 meters of you, and give your guests instructions inbetween firing.

So we ran the gamut of the assessments as drills, and that was the week in Makuleke, over in no-time at all.
